🧠 Clinical Judgment

The six thinking steps the NCLEX actually scores — and 570 questions sorted by which step they test, so you can drill the one you keep missing.

⚠️ Why you miss these even when you know the content

Clinical judgment questions rarely fail you on facts. They fail you because you answer a different step than the one being asked.

You read a scenario, spot the problem, and jump straight to what would I do — so you pick an intervention when the question wanted you to say what the finding means, or to say which client is worst. The intervention you picked is often a perfectly good intervention. It is just not the answer to the question asked.

Before you look at the options, name the step. Say it out loud if you can: "this is asking me to evaluate." Then answer only that step. This one habit is worth more than any content review.

The six steps

1

Recognize cues

What matters here?

Sounds like
  • "Which finding should the nurse report?"
  • "Which findings are consistent with…?"
  • "Which data is relevant to this client?"
  • "Which finding is expected for…?"
How to answer

Sort the data into expected and not expected. Do not explain it and do not fix it — just point at what is off.

Worked exampleA client on lithium reports vomiting and diarrhea for two days and has a coarse hand tremor.
→ Asks which findings to report → persistent GI losses and a coarse tremor. A fine tremor at the start is expected; this is not.
2

Analyze cues

What does it mean?

Sounds like
  • "These findings indicate…"
  • "The nurse interprets this as…"
  • "Which condition do these suggest?"
  • A lab value or ABG followed by "this means"
How to answer

Give the finding a name. The answer is a problem or a diagnosis, never an action. If every option is something you would do, you misread the step.

Worked example"The nurse interprets these findings as indicating:"
→ Name it: lithium toxicity. The answer is the problem, not "hold the dose."
3

Prioritize hypotheses

Who is worst, or what is most likely?

Sounds like
  • "Which client should the nurse see first?"
  • "Which client is at greatest risk?"
  • "What is the most likely cause?"
  • "Which complication is most likely?"
How to answer

Rank. Unstable and unexpected beats sick but expected. Airway, breathing, circulation win. A client who is deteriorating beats one who is stably bad.

Worked exampleFour clients: one voicing hopelessness, one with a plan and the pills saved up, one pacing and irritable, one refusing group.
→ Rank: a plan plus the means is the most acute risk. In psych, a physiological emergency such as NMS would outrank all four.
4

Generate solutions

What is the goal?

Sounds like
  • "Which outcome is appropriate?"
  • "The plan of care should include…"
  • "Which goal is realistic for this client?"
How to answer

Pick the goal, not the task. A real outcome says what the client will do, how much, and by when. Anything starting "The nurse will" is usually the trap.

Worked exampleA client with anorexia nervosa is admitted. "Which outcome is appropriate?"
→ Goal: "The client will gain 1 to 2 pounds per week." Measurable and time-bound, not "the client will eat better."
5

Take action

What do I do?

Sounds like
  • "Which action should the nurse take first?"
  • "What should the nurse do next?"
  • "Which statement should the nurse make?"
  • "Which task can be delegated?"
How to answer

Assess before you intervene — unless the client is unstable, then act. Do not pick "notify the provider" if there is something you must do or check first.

Worked exampleA client says, "I have nothing left to live for." "Which response should the nurse make?"
→ Do something: ask directly whether they have a plan to harm themselves. Asking does not plant the idea.
6

Evaluate outcomes

Did it work?

Sounds like
  • "Indicates the teaching was effective"
  • "Indicates the client understands"
  • "Needs further teaching" (reversed!)
  • "Indicates the medication is working"
How to answer

Something already happened; judge the result. Read the last six words twice — "needs further teaching" means you are hunting for the wrong statement.

Worked exampleA client is taking an MAOI. "Which statement indicates the teaching was effective?"
→ Judge the result: "I will avoid aged cheese, cured meats, and draft beer."

🚨 The traps that cost you the most points

1. Answering "take action" when it asked "analyze"

The single most common miss. If the stem ends in indicates, suggests, or the nurse interprets, the answer is a name for the problem, not a thing you do.

2. Picking the intervention you would do second

On a "first" question, several options are things you genuinely would do. Assess before you intervene — unless the client is unstable, in which case act first. Never pick "notify the provider" if there is something you must assess or do in the same second.

3. Treating "expected" as "urgent"

On "which client first," the sick-looking-but-expected client is bait. A day-two temperature of 100.2 F is expected. A new, unexpected, or worsening finding wins — especially airway, breathing, or circulation.

4. Missing the reversal

"Which statement indicates the client needs FURTHER teaching" means you are hunting for the wrong statement. Read the last six words of every stem twice.

5. Nurse-centered goals

On "generate solutions," anything starting with "The nurse will…" is almost always wrong. A goal describes what the client will do, by when.
